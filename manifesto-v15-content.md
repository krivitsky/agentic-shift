Something is being sold to you alongside the models. Not only the tools — a set of working habits, arriving as advice. Generate more. Review less. Let the craft go.

We don't think this is a conspiracy. We think it's a good strategy. When your product is a frontier model, one route to product-market fit is to change the market rather than the product — and habits are part of the market. The advice is real advice, and it is also doing strategic work. Both at once.

It is perhaps too early to write manifestos; the ground is still shifting. We'd rather name what we already see, and what we won't trade away.

## 1. What we optimize for

Everyone is optimizing for something.

The frontier labs optimize for adoption and throughput. That is legitimate, and it is their business — not yours.

We optimize for two things: the value delivered to customers, and the health of the organization that delivers it — its capacity to keep learning, keep judging, and keep changing direction. Where those two diverge from adoption and throughput, we follow ours.

## 2. Who sets the direction

Direction is not something you receive.

Which capabilities you grow, and which you are content to rent, is a design decision. It gets made either way — deliberately, or by default by whoever adopts fastest. We think it belongs with leadership, made before the tools arrive rather than after.

## 3. What we refuse

We do not treat the loss of judgment as progress.

We do not accept dependence as a design outcome.

We do not confuse volume of output with capability.

---

Each shift has a half that nobody sells you. That half is the work.

### 1. features → systems
Product engineers stop shipping one-off features and build agentic systems — self-improving loops that turn intent into working solutions, with someone accountable for the loop, not only for its output.
*Skip that accountability and you get more output and the same product.*

### 2. gatekeeping → guardrails
Product engineers stop inspecting for quality at the end and engineer it into the system — the tests, the specifications, the coverage, the harness that makes output trustworthy before anyone reads it.
*Remove the inspection without building the harness and you ship faster for two quarters, then cannot explain your own incidents.*

### 3. specialists → craftspeople
Product engineers shift from narrow, assembly-line specialists to end-to-end craftspeople who pull in specialist depth on demand and grow from it — the human is the one acting, not the one being filled in for.
*Skip the growing and you have swapped narrow specialists for generalists with no way to tell what they are missing.*

### 4. proxies → users
No more telephone game through manager-proxies, no more sitting in the basement — product engineers work at zero distance from customers and users. That takes an org structure that permits it: a mandate, not an encouragement.
*Skip the mandate and you deliver the wrong thing faster.*

### 5. output → outcomes
Product engineers stop being measured by output and own the outer loop — sensing drift and correcting course themselves, which takes the mandate to change direction.
*Without it you get outcome language over output management, and nothing changes but the vocabulary.*
